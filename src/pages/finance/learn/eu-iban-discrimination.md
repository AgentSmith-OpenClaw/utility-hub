# EU IBAN Discrimination Is Still Happening: Here's How to Fight Back

It’s 2026, and despite the Single Euro Payments Area (SEPA) regulations being crystal clear, IBAN discrimination is still a massive headache across Europe. You open a sleek new account with an N26, Revolut, or Bunq, and you get a Spanish, Lithuanian, or Dutch IBAN. You live in France or Germany, and suddenly your employer tells you they can’t pay your salary into a "foreign" account. Or your gym refuses to set up a direct debit.

This is illegal. Full stop. Article 9 of the SEPA Regulation (EU) No 260/2012 strictly prohibits this behavior. But companies do it anyway because it’s easier for their outdated payroll systems, and they assume you won't fight back.

## Why Companies Still Discriminate

The reality is simple: laziness and legacy tech. Many local utility companies, gyms, and even government offices use ancient software that only validates domestic IBAN formats. A German IBAN starts with DE and has 22 characters. A Spanish one starts with ES and has 24. Their system throws an error, and the underpaid HR rep tells you "it’s not possible."

They aren't doing it to be malicious; they’re doing it because fixing their IT infrastructure costs money, and rejecting your non-local IBAN is free. Until you make it expensive for them.

## The Playbook for Beating IBAN Discrimination

You don't have to accept this. You have the law on your side, but you need to know how to use it. Here is the exact process I use when a company tries to reject a legitimate SEPA IBAN.

1. **Quote the Law Immediately:** Don't argue with customer service reps about company policy. Send them a template email citing Article 9 of the SEPA Regulation. Tell them they have 7 days to process the direct debit or salary payment. If they refuse, you escalate.
2. **Report Them to the National Competent Authority (NCA):** Every EU country has an NCA responsible for enforcing SEPA rules. In Germany, it's the BaFin. In France, the Banque de France. Use our [Toolisk IBAN Discrimination Reporter](/tools/eu-sepa-complaint-generator) to instantly generate a formal complaint letter localized for your specific country's authority. This is the only thing that actually scares these companies.
3. **Use the "Accept My IBAN" Network:** There is a coordinated effort across Europe to publicly shame and fine companies that do this. Submit your case to acceptmyiban.org, which aggregates these complaints and forwards them directly to the European Commission.

## Don't Switch Accounts Just to Please Them

The worst thing you can do is give in and open a local brick-and-mortar bank account just to get your internet set up. That reinforces the bad behavior and costs you money in monthly fees. 

The SEPA zone was designed to make banking borderless. Your Lithuanian or Spanish IBAN is just as valid in Berlin as a local one. Force them to comply. It might take an extra email or a formal complaint, but the law is entirely on your side. Stand your ground.
