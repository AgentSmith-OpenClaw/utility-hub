# Salary to Hourly Wage Conversion: Maximizing Your Earning Potential

A $75,000 salary sounds great until you realize you are working 60 hours a week. Suddenly, your "great salary" breaks down to an hourly rate that barely beats a part-time retail job. 

Understanding how to convert your annual salary into an actual hourly wage is the most powerful tool you have for negotiating raises, evaluating job offers, or deciding to go freelance.

## The Basic Calculation

The standard work year in the US, Canada, and the UK is generally considered to be 2,080 hours (40 hours a week x 52 weeks). 

To find your gross hourly rate, divide your salary by 2,080.
**$75,000 / 2,080 = $36.05 per hour**

However, this is just the baseline. To get the *true* picture, you need to make adjustments using our [Salary to Hourly Converter](/finance/calculators/salary-to-hourly).

## Adjusting for Reality

**1. Unpaid Overtime**
Salaried employees often work more than 40 hours. If you regularly work 50 hours a week, you are working 2,600 hours a year. 
**$75,000 / 2,600 = $28.84 per hour.** 
That promotion that required "extra dedication" just resulted in a pay cut to your hourly rate.

**2. Paid Time Off (PTO) and Holidays**
In the EU and UK, workers often get 25-30 days of paid leave plus public holidays. In the US, 10-15 days is more common. If you actually only work 46 weeks a year (1,840 hours), your hourly rate goes *up*. 
**$75,000 / 1,840 = $40.76 per hour.**

**3. The Commute and Unpaid Lunch**
As we discussed in our Commute Cost Guide, if you spend an hour commuting and an hour on an unpaid lunch break where you are tethered to the office, your 8-hour workday is actually 10 hours of your life dedicated to your job. Factor those hours in, and your rate drops significantly.

## Why This Matters

**Evaluating Job Offers:** Company A offers $80,000 with the expectation of 50-hour weeks. Company B offers $70,000 with a strict 35-hour week (common in parts of Europe) and fully remote work. On an hourly basis, Company B pays significantly more.

**Freelancing:** If you are transitioning to freelance work, you cannot simply charge your old hourly rate. You have to account for self-employment taxes, health insurance, and unpaid administrative time. A freelancer usually needs to charge double their salaried hourly rate just to break even. Stop looking at your gross annual salary as a status symbol. Time is your most valuable asset.
