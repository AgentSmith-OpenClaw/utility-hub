# The Reality of High-Yield Savings Accounts for US and UK Expats in 2026

I spend half my time looking at banking structures, and frankly, expats get the worst deal imaginable. If you live in London but file taxes in the US, or vice-versa, your banking options shrink. Most domestic banks don't want the compliance headache. But you still need a place to park cash that beats inflation.

Here is the straight truth about high-yield savings accounts (HYSAs) for cross-border professionals right now. 

## The US Expat Problem: FATCA and Ghosting

American citizens living abroad know the drill. The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) makes foreign banks treat you like a liability. Many UK and European banks will outright refuse to open an account for a US citizen. 

Even worse, your US-based banks often freeze accounts if they detect a foreign residential address. You end up stuck in a middle ground, earning 0.01% on legacy checking accounts because you are terrified to move the money.

### How to Fix It

**1. Maintain a US Address (Legally)**
Use a family member's address or a specialized mail-forwarding service designed for expats. This keeps your US-based HYSA active. Institutions like Ally or Marcus check residency during onboarding, but they rarely investigate further unless your IP address is consistently foreign and you make erratic transfers. (Pro tip: use a VPN when checking balances).

**2. State Department Federal Credit Union (SDFCU)**
This is the open secret for US expats. You don't need to work for the government to join—you can join via the American Consumer Council. SDFCU explicitly accepts foreign addresses, offers decent share certificate rates, and won't freeze your account for living in Berlin.

## The UK Expat Problem: The Non-Resident Trap

If you leave the UK, you lose access to Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs). You can keep existing ones, but you cannot add new funds. And most UK retail banks offer terrible rates on standard saver accounts for non-residents.

### Your Best Moves

**1. Offshore Banking (The Isle of Man Route)**
Barclays, Lloyds, and NatWest all operate offshore branches in the Channel Islands or the Isle of Man. These are designed for expats. The catch? The minimum deposits are high—usually £25,000 to £100,000. The interest rates track the Bank of England base rate closely. If you have the cash, this is the most secure route.

**2. Multi-Currency FinTechs**
Revolut and Wise are not banks in every jurisdiction, but they offer "interest vaults" or "interest jars" through partner banks. For smaller balances, these work fine. But I wouldn't trust them with a house deposit. Their customer service is notoriously bad when an account gets flagged.

## Tax Implications: The Double Hit

Earning interest is great until you realize both countries want a cut. 

If you are a US citizen in the UK:
- The UK taxes the interest (subject to your Personal Savings Allowance).
- The US taxes the interest (because the US taxes global income).
- You have to claim Foreign Tax Credits on your IRS Form 1116 to avoid double taxation.

If you are a UK citizen in the US:
- The US taxes the interest.
- The UK generally does not, assuming you are formally non-resident for tax purposes.

Do not ignore this. The IRS penalty for failing to file an FBAR (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts) starts at $10,000 per violation. It is a brutal fine for a simple mistake.

## The Bottom Line

Stop leaving money in zero-interest current accounts. If you are an expat, you have to work harder to find yield, but it exists. Get an SDFCU account if you are American. Look at the Isle of Man if you are British. Sort your taxes out early.
